:::danger[Safety]
Maintenance jobs put your hands on blades, edges, and batteries. Unplug the tool or remove the battery before cleaning, adjusting, or changing anything, keep cutting edges pointed away from you when you clean or sharpen them, and wear eye protection for anything involving abrasives, solvents, or sparks. Never use flammable or combustible solvents around power tools, and never open a battery pack or attempt electrical repairs (tool makers and CCOHS both say these belong to qualified technicians). Model-specific limits, intervals, and settings come from the operator's manual for your exact tool. This guide does not replace hands-on instruction.
:::

## The one-line version

**Choosing a tool is choosing a boot, and maintaining it is lacing, cleaning, and drying it.** A boot that fits the trail and is cared for keeps you upright. One that is the wrong kind, or neglected, fails at the worst moment. OSHA's first two rules for hand and power tools say the same: keep tools in good condition with regular maintenance, and use the right tool for the job.

## Choosing between options

### Hand tool or power tool

Both are legitimate. OSHA's booklet notes that the greatest hazards of hand tools come from misuse and poor maintenance, while power tools must be fitted with guards and safety switches and are extremely hazardous when used improperly. Choose by the job and by the hazards you are ready to manage.

| Factor | Hand tool | Power tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What can go wrong | Slips, loose or damaged heads and handles, dull edges | All of that, plus guards, kickback, electric shock or battery fire, and noise |
| Extra protective equipment | Eye protection at minimum | Eye protection and often hearing and dust protection (Milwaukee manuals; CCOHS) |
| Extra habits | Clamp the work; keep edges sharp | Unplug or remove the battery for every change; keep cords and dust under control |
| Model-specific specs | Few (size, thread, grade) | Many: speeds, capacities, torque settings, blade speed ratings; all in the manual |
| Skill it builds (this guide's view) | Feel for grain, angle, and pressure | Control of speed and support of the work |

This guide's rule of thumb (not from a single source): learn the hand-tool version of a skill first when a hand-tool version exists, such as sawing a line by hand before running a circular saw. You feel what the tool does before a motor multiplies it.

### Corded or cordless

| Factor | Corded | Cordless |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrical hazard | Shock: needs grounding, double insulation, or a GFCI (OSHA, CCOHS); cords must be inspected and kept clear | Battery fire and thermal runaway if the battery is damaged, defective, or mistreated (New York State guide) |
| Set-up chores | Cord routing, outlet, and extension cord rated for the use | Battery charging, charger matching, and battery storage |
| Condition to watch | The cord: cracking and fraying (CCOHS) | The battery: charge level (Milwaukee's saw manual lists a low battery among causes of a binding, smoking blade) and case damage |
| Changing accessories | Unplug | Remove the battery (Milwaukee) |
| Stated power and runtime | Model-specific | Model-specific |

Neither is "safer". They swap hazards. If you cannot charge and store batteries responsibly (a cool, dry place, not on a couch, not unattended), a corded tool avoids that hazard. If you cannot keep a cord out of the cut and out of walkways, a cordless tool avoids that one.

### Match the tool to the job

| Job | Tool | Why, and the catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plunge cut in the middle of a panel | Jig saw or reciprocating saw | Milwaukee's circular saw manual says it recommends those over a circular saw for plunge cuts, and calls for "extreme caution" if you must use a circular saw |
| Cutting a curve | Jig saw with a narrow blade | OSHA's jig-saw page: narrower blades for tight turns, and make turns gradually |
| Cutting with the grain on a narrow board | Rip-tooth handsaw, or circular saw with a rip fence | TM 9-867 says rip teeth act like chisels; Milwaukee's manual says to use a rip fence for rips 4 inches wide or less |
| Cutting metal or masonry with a circular saw | Avoid for anything continuous | Milwaukee: its saws are not meant for continuous metal or masonry cutting; if you do, use the right blade and reserve and mark the saw for that use because dust and grit can make the guard hang up |
| Driving many screws | Drill/driver with an adjustable clutch | Milwaukee: a properly adjusted clutch slips at a preset torque so the screw is not driven too deep; the settings are only a guide, so test on scrap |
| Striking a hardened steel tool or surface | A soft hammer, or a block of copper, brass, fiber, or wood between | TM 9-867: hardened steel can shed sharp pieces when struck with a hammer |
| Turning a nut or bolt | Wrench of the correct size | CCOHS and TM 9-867: pliers round the corners |
| Holding the work | Clamp or vise | OSHA: frees both hands |

### What to look for when you buy

- **Certification and reputation.** For anything with a battery, the New York State guide says to buy from reputable makers, look for a safety mark from a nationally recognized testing laboratory, and check for recalls before buying. Health Canada names marks such as CSA, cUL, cETL, and UL.
- **Good quality.** CCOHS's general hand-tool page says to opt for good quality tools, and to use tools designed so the wrist can stay straight.
- **The manual is available.** Tool makers publish operator's manuals online (Milwaukee's are the examples used across this guide). Read the manual before you buy, because it tells you the capacities, warnings, and service intervals.
- **Capacity that matches your job.** CCOHS warns against exceeding a drill's drilling capacity limits. Milwaukee's drill manual lists maximum sizes by material as the example of how a capacity table looks; your tool has its own.

## Maintaining tools

### Before every use

OSHA's third rule: examine each tool for damage before use, and do not use damaged tools. Use this list:

| Tool | Look for |
| --- | --- |
| Any hand tool | Cracked or splintered handles; damaged edges; rust; dull cutting edges (CCOHS) |
| Hammers | Loose head on the handle; mushroomed or chipped face; cracks in the claw or eye (CCOHS, OSHA) |
| Chisels and punches | Mushroomed or chipped heads, chipped edges, bent or cracked blades (CCOHS, OSHA) |
| Wrenches and pliers | Spread or worn jaws, damaged box-wrench points, worn teeth (CCOHS) |
| Screwdrivers | Rounded tips, split handles, bent shafts (CCOHS) |
| Saws | Missing or bent teeth, rust, a bent or sprung blade (TM 9-867, CCOHS) |
| Clamps | Bent frames or bent spindles (CCOHS) |
| Corded tools | Cord cracks and fraying; damaged plug; a switch that does not turn the tool on and off (Milwaukee: such a tool must be repaired) |
| Any power tool | Guards moving freely; blade or bit sharp, straight, and seated; nothing loose (Milwaukee, CCOHS) |
| Batteries | Cracked, swollen, or dented case; anything hot or smelly (New York guide; Health Canada) |

If something fails the check: OSHA says damaged portable electric tools should be removed from use and tagged "Do Not Use", and CCOHS says to replace or repair defective equipment.

### After every use

**Hand tools (from TM 9-867, the 1945 U.S. War Department manual, and CCOHS).**

1. Wipe off dirt, sawdust, and moisture. Keep tools clean and dry.
2. If rust has started, scour it off with a fine abrasive cloth (TM 9-867 names crocus cloth and fine aluminum oxide cloth).
3. Coat bare metal with a thin film of oil or rust-preventive compound (TM 9-867), and store in a dry place. For a combination square, Starrett suggests a light film of petroleum jelly on exposed surfaces for extended storage.

TM 9-867's cleaner is called dry-cleaning solvent, a term from the 1940s. Do not assume any solvent in your home is the same or safe. For **power tools**, Milwaukee's manuals say to use only mild soap and a damp cloth, because gasoline, turpentine, lacquer thinner, paint thinner, chlorinated cleaning solvents, ammonia, and household detergents containing ammonia damage plastics and insulated parts, and never to use flammable or combustible solvents around tools.

**Power tools (Milwaukee manuals, as examples; CCOHS).**

1. Unplug the tool or remove the battery.
2. Clean dust and debris from the vents, and keep the motor free of accumulated dust and chips (CCOHS).
3. Keep handles clean, dry, and free of oil and grease.
4. Never immerse a tool, battery, or charger in liquid or let liquid run inside it.
5. Inspect for undue noise, misalignment, binding, or breakage. Milwaukee's manuals say to return the tool, battery pack, and charger to a service facility for inspection after six months to one year depending on use. That interval is for those models; check yours.

### Sharpening: the principle

A cutting edge is where two faces meet. Sharpening grinds both faces until they meet cleanly at a line, like wearing down two sides of a wedge until they come to a point again.

- **Sharp is safer.** CCOHS says dull chisels are hard to control and says to keep cutting tools sharp; Milwaukee says sharp, clean cutting tools are less likely to bind and easier to control; OSHA says dull knives and scissors cause more hazards than sharp ones.
- **Both faces matter.** TM 9-867's chisel and plane procedure hones the bevel on an oilstone, then lays the flat back on the stone to remove the wire edge (the thin curl of steel raised along the edge), and repeats until sharp. Stropping finishes a very keen edge.
- **The bevel angle is a shape you keep.** TM 9-867 says a bevel should stay flat or slightly concave, never rounded, and should be about twice the thickness of the blade near the edge.
- **Heat destroys steel.** TM 9-867 says keep chisels and plane irons cool when grinding, by dipping them in water often, because heat softens the steel. CCOHS says not to use grinders on heat-treated tools and to use whetstones instead.
- **A guide holds the angle.** Lie-Nielsen's honing guide sets the angle by how far the blade projects from the front of the guide, and lists a projection for each angle. Yours will have its own numbers.

### Storing tools

| Tool | How to store it | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chisels | In a rack, roll, or slotted pouch, edges protected from other metal; bevel side up on the bench; caps on the edges | TM 9-867; CCOHS |
| Saws | Hung up or teeth protected, never loose in a toolbox or on the floor | TM 9-867; CCOHS |
| Planes | Laid on their side, never face down, with the iron fully withdrawn into the plane | TM 9-867 |
| C-clamps | In a rack rather than a drawer | CCOHS |
| Small hand tools | In a sturdy toolbox; not left on high surfaces; not carried in pockets | CCOHS |
| Metal tools in leather | For long periods, Lie-Nielsen advises against storing bare metal in leather cases, and suggests a thick coat of oil and wrapping in cloth | Lie-Nielsen FAQ |
| Power tools | Dry place, disconnected, out of reach of children | CCOHS; Milwaukee |
| Batteries | Cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight and metal objects such as keys, nails, and coins; not in hot or cold vehicles; about 50 percent charge for extended storage of larger systems, checked every three months (Health Canada) | New York guide; Health Canada; Milwaukee |

## Common mistakes

- **Putting tools away wet or dirty.** Rust starts in a night. TM 9-867 and CCOHS both call for clean, dry storage.
- **Overheating an edge on a grinder.** It cannot be undone, because the steel goes soft.
- **Buying by the number on the box.** Speeds, torque, and capacity vary by model; read your own manual.
- **Keeping a damaged tool "for light jobs".** OSHA's fix is to tag it and take it out of use.
- **Charging batteries on a couch or overnight unattended.** See [power tool hazards](/tools/concepts/power-tool-hazards/).
