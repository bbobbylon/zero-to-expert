:::danger[Safety]
Power tools cut, spin, shock, and (with batteries) burn. The controls, in order of importance: keep every guard in place and working, hold the tool with both hands and stand out of the line of the blade, clamp the work, disconnect the plug or remove the battery before any adjustment or accessory change, keep cords and dust away from the cut, and use only the charger made for your battery. Model-specific settings, limits, and warnings come from the operator's manual for your exact tool; the manuals quoted here are examples read on 2026-09-26, not a substitute for yours. This guide does not replace hands-on instruction.
:::

## The one-line version

A power tool swaps your muscle for a motor. It keeps its force when you flinch and does not know you are there, so **every protection on it is a substitute for a reflex you do not have fast enough.** Think of a bicycle and a motorcycle: the balance skill is the same, but the energy is higher, the reaction time is shorter, and mistakes cost more.

OSHA's tool booklet puts it in one sentence: power tools must be fitted with guards and safety switches, and they are extremely hazardous when used improperly.

## How the protections work

### Guards: the blender lid

A guard is like the lid on a blender. The machine still spins; the lid keeps you away from the part that hurts. OSHA says exposed moving parts must be guarded, and that guards protect against the point of operation, in-running nip points, rotating parts, and flying chips and sparks. Safety guards must never be removed while a tool is in use.

For a portable circular saw with a blade larger than 2 inches, OSHA requires an upper guard that covers the entire blade and a retractable lower guard that covers the teeth except where they meet the work, and that automatically returns to cover the blade when the saw is pulled from the work. Milwaukee's manual (an example manual) adds the rules a user needs:

- Check that the lower guard closes properly before each use, and do not use the saw if it does not move freely and close instantly.
- Never clamp or tie the lower guard open.
- Retract the lower guard by hand only for special cuts such as plunge cuts, and release it as soon as the blade enters the material.
- Check that the lower guard is covering the blade before you put the saw down, because a coasting blade with no guard makes the saw walk backward and cut whatever is in its path.

CCOHS adds: keep guards clean and free of sawdust, and never hold or force the guard open.

The Milwaukee manual also describes a guard test for that saw (battery out, saw on its side, push the guard fully back into the housing and release it, and it should spring back at once). Every model has its own procedure. Look in your manual.

### Switches: the dead-man's switch

A constant-pressure switch is a "hold to run" switch, like the dead-man's handle on a train that stops the train if the driver lets go. OSHA says these hand-held tools must have a constant-pressure switch that shuts off power when pressure is released: drills, fastener drivers, belt sanders, larger grinders and disc sanders, reciprocating, saber, scroll, and jig saws with blade shanks wider than 1/4 inch, circular saws with blades over 2 inches, and chain saws. A lock-on control is allowed only if the worker can shut the tool off in a single motion using the same finger. OSHA's fact sheet says never to disable the pressure switch or control, such as by clamping a hand-held grinder in a vise.

The same principle governs how you carry a tool. Milwaukee's manual: make sure the switch is off before you connect power or a battery, pick up the tool, or carry it, because carrying a tool with a finger on the switch invites accidents.

### Grounding and GFCIs: the plumber who counts water

Electric shock is one of the most serious hazards of corded tools. OSHA says even a small amount of current can cause the heart to fibrillate, and a shock can also make you fall off a ladder. Corded electric tools must have a three-wire cord with a ground plugged into a grounded outlet, be double insulated, or be powered by a low-voltage isolation transformer. Never remove the third prong, and any adapter used on a two-hole outlet must have its wire attached to a known ground. CCOHS adds: test tools for effective grounding with a continuity tester or GFCI, and stop using a tool that tingles, since that indicates a fault.

A **ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI)** is like a plumber counting the water going into a pipe and the water coming back. If a cup goes missing, something is leaking, so the plumber shuts the valve at once. Specifically, OSHA says a GFCI compares the current going to and returning from the equipment, and interrupts power when they differ by about 5 milliamperes, in as little as 1/40 of a second. Every GFCI has test and reset buttons. It does not protect you from line contact hazards such as touching two live wires.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Outdoors | Use an extension cord rated for outdoor use (CCOHS: marked "W-A" or "W") | CCOHS; Milwaukee manual |

## Kickback: the watermelon seed

**Kickback** is what a saw does when its blade is pinched: like squeezing a slippery watermelon seed between two fingers, the pinched object shoots out. The Milwaukee manual defines it as a sudden reaction to a pinched, jammed, or misaligned blade that lifts an uncontrolled saw up and out of the workpiece toward you, and describes three mechanisms:

1. The **kerf closes** on the blade, pinching it. The blade stalls and the motor's reaction drives the saw back toward you.
2. The blade becomes **twisted or misaligned** in the cut.
3. Teeth at the **back edge of the blade dig into the top surface** of the wood, making the blade climb out of the kerf and jump back toward you.

The manual's prevention rules line up with the causes:

| Cause | Prevention (Milwaukee manual; CCOHS agrees) |
| --- | --- |
| Kerf closing on the blade | Support the work so it cannot sag or pinch; support large panels on both sides near the cut line and near the edge. Use a rip fence or guide when ripping. |
| Twisted blade | Never twist the saw to change or check alignment (CCOHS). Keep the shoe flat on the work and do not force the cut. |
| Dull or badly set blade | Do not use dull or damaged blades: they make a narrow kerf, extra friction, and binding. |
| Blade depth or bevel shifting | Depth and bevel locking levers must be tight before you cut. |
| Hidden objects | Use extra caution sawing into walls or blind areas; check for nails and screws first (CCOHS). |
| Where you stand | Hold with both hands, position your arms to resist the force, and stand to either side of the blade, not in line with it. |
| When the blade binds or you stop mid-cut | Release the trigger and hold the saw still in the material until the blade stops completely. Never pull the saw back while the blade is moving. Find and fix the cause. |
| Restarting in the cut | Center the blade in the kerf and make sure the teeth are not engaged in the material. |

Jig saws and reciprocating saws can kick back or stall too. CCOHS's rule: make sure the blade is not in contact with the material before you start the saw, keep the shoe (base plate) firmly on the stock, and do not force the saw around curves. OSHA's jig-saw page adds that turning should be gradual, and that narrower blades suit tight turns.

Drills have their own version: a bit that jams can twist the tool in your hands. The Milwaukee drill manual says to reduce pressure if the tool begins to stall so the bit regains speed, and to reverse the motor if the bit binds. CCOHS says never to free a jammed bit by starting and stopping the drill; disconnect the tool and then remove the bit. Milwaukee also says to hold or brace the drill securely.

## Blades and bits: right one, on right, rated right

| Rule | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use only accessories the maker recommends for that tool | Others may be hazardous. OSHA specifically warns that a saw blade can fit an abrasive-wheel tool without being safe, and can cause loss of control, cuts, and amputations. | OSHA 3950; Milwaukee manuals |
| Match the accessory's speed rating to the tool | Milwaukee's saw manual says use only blades with a maximum safe operating speed greater than the tool's no-load RPM on its nameplate, and accessories rated at least as high as nameplate RPM. The value is on **your** tool. | Milwaukee saw manual |
| Match the blade's arbor hole and use the right washers and bolt | A blade that does not match the mounting hardware runs off-center and causes loss of control. | Milwaukee saw manual |
| Install blades with the teeth pointing the way the tool's arrow shows | The wrong way round: Milwaukee's troubleshooting list includes "blade is on backwards" as a cause of binding and burning. | Milwaukee saw manual |
| Keep bits and blades sharp; do not use bent bits | Dull tools bind, and bent bits vibrate. | CCOHS; Milwaukee manuals |
| Tighten the chuck fully and remove the chuck key before starting | An unseated bit turns off-center. | CCOHS; Milwaukee drill manual |
| Set the depth so only a little blade shows below the work | CCOHS: the lowest tooth of a circular saw should not go more than about 1/8 in (0.3 cm) below the wood; a jig saw blade should extend no more than 1/8 to 1/4 in beyond the material. Milwaukee's saw manual says less than a full tooth should show and mentions 1/8 to 1/4 in. Follow your manual. | CCOHS; Milwaukee manual |
| Disconnect the plug or remove the battery for every blade or bit change | The tool cannot start if it has no power. | OSHA; CCOHS; Milwaukee manuals |

## Batteries: energy in a small box

A lithium-ion battery stores a lot of energy in a small space. The New York State guide explains that if the battery is damaged, defective, uncertified, or treated badly, it can overheat in a chain reaction called **thermal runaway**, which causes violent fires. Think of a stack of dominoes set so close that once one falls they all go.

**Buying and using**

- Buy from reputable makers and look for a safety mark from a nationally recognized testing lab (New York's guide names Underwriters Laboratories; Health Canada lists CSA, cUL, cETL, and UL). Check for recalls before you buy (New York's guide points to cpsc.gov/Recalls).
- Use only the charger that came with the tool or one the tool maker approves. Milwaukee's manual: a charger suited to one pack may create a fire risk with another pack, and tools should only be used with their designated battery packs.
- Never disassemble or modify a battery. Do not use a damaged one: Milwaukee's manual says damaged or modified batteries may exhibit unpredictable behavior resulting in fire, explosion, or injury, and says never to service damaged packs yourself.
- Keep a pack you are not using away from paper clips, coins, keys, nails, screws, and other small metal objects that could bridge the terminals; shorting the terminals can cause burns or a fire.
- If liquid leaks from a battery, avoid contact. Milwaukee's manual says to flush with water and, if it gets in the eyes, to seek medical help as well.

**Charging**

- Never leave a charging battery unattended, and remove it once it is full. Plug the charger straight into a wall outlet, not a power strip.
- Charge on a flat, dry surface away from children, direct sunlight, liquids, and tripping hazards. Health Canada adds: away from soft surfaces such as couches and beds, and away from exit doors.
- New York's guide says not to charge below 32°F (0°C) or above 105°F. Your tool's manual states its own range, and it wins.

**Storing**

- Store in a cool, dry place out of direct sunlight, and check batteries regularly for cracked cases (New York). Health Canada advises never leaving batteries in hot or cold vehicles and, for larger systems, storing at about 50 percent charge for long periods and checking every three months.

> TODO(source): the charge level and storage temperature your tool maker recommends for long storage. Read the manual or charger instructions; Health Canada's 50 percent figure is for larger battery systems in general, not a specific tool.

**Warning signs of a failing battery** (New York's guide): it feels extremely hot, swells or bulges, hisses, cracks or pops, smells strongly or unusually, or smokes. If you see one, stop using the device, turn it off, unplug it, and, if it is safe, move it away from anything flammable using tongs or gloves, then leave the area and call 9-1-1. If a battery catches fire, do not try to put it out. The guide says lithium-ion fires spread quickly and can reignite, water may not stop them, and fire extinguishers do not work on them, so the safest choice is to leave and call 9-1-1.

**Disposal.** Both New York and Health Canada say not to put lithium-ion batteries in household trash. Take them to a battery take-back or hazardous-waste program (New York names Call2Recycle's collection program; Health Canada says to contact your municipality).

## Cord versus battery: which hazards you are trading

| | Corded tool | Cordless tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main electrical hazard | Shock; needs grounding, double insulation, or a GFCI (OSHA, CCOHS) | Battery fire and thermal runaway (New York guide) |
| Hazard in the work area | Cord in the cutting path or a trip hazard | A battery left in a bag with metal objects, or charging unattended |
| How it starts by accident | Plugged in with the switch on | Battery latched in with the switch or trigger lock not set |
| Making it safe to change accessories | Unplug | Remove the battery (Milwaukee: lock the trigger or remove the pack) |
| Weak-power symptom | A long, thin, or damaged extension cord | A low battery. Milwaukee's saw manual lists "battery charge is low" among the causes of a blade binding, smoking, or turning blue |

## Common mistakes

- **Propping the guard open "just this once."** The guard is the only thing between the blade and your leg when you set the saw down.
- **Standing behind the blade.** Kickback travels back along the blade's line. Stand to the side.
- **Forcing the tool.** Milwaukee's manuals and CCOHS both say not to force the tool. Milwaukee's saw manual names forcing as a cause of kickback, and both manuals warn that continuous overloading may permanently damage the tool or battery pack.
- **Walking around with a finger on the trigger.**
- **Charging on the couch overnight.**
- **Cutting unsupported work.** A sagging board pinches the blade.
