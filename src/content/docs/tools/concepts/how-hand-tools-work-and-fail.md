:::danger[Safety]
Hand tools cut, strike, and slip. The greatest hazards, according to OSHA, come from misuse and poor maintenance: a chisel used as a screwdriver can snap and fly, a loose hammer head can leave its handle, a sprung wrench can slip, and a mushroomed striking face can shatter. Wear eye protection whenever you strike, cut, or pry, clamp the work so both hands are on the tool, keep your body out of the tool's path in case it slips, and throw out or repair damaged tools. This page is general guidance; it does not replace hands-on instruction from someone experienced.
:::

## The one-line version

Almost every hand tool is one of three machines: a **lever** (wrench, pliers, hammer), a **wedge** (chisel, saw tooth, screw thread), or a **gauge** (tape, square) that carries a length or an angle from one place to another. A tool works when its shape matches the job the way a key matches a lock, and it fails when you ask it to be a different machine than the one it was built as. A screwdriver is a bad pry bar for the same reason a house key is a bad screwdriver: it can be forced to do it, but something bends.

OSHA's tool booklet says it plainly: the greatest hazards of hand tools come from misuse and improper maintenance.

## How it works, family by family

### Measuring and marking: tools that carry a length

A **tape measure** carries a length; a **combination square** carries a right angle (and a 45-degree angle). Their whole value is that they are honest. A crooked line or a wrong number gets cut into the wood and stays there, so treat them as the most delicate tools you own.

- **Reading inches.** The Bellevue College woodworking handout explains that each inch is divided in halves, then again into quarters, eighths, sixteenths, thirty-seconds, and sixty-fourths, and that longer lines mark bigger fractions. Read a measurement as whole inches plus the leftover fraction, and reduce fractions (4/16 becomes 1/4). [Walkthrough 1](/tools/walkthroughs/1-read-a-tape-and-mark-a-cut/) practices this.
- **The loose hook.** The metal hook on the end of a tape is meant to move slightly. As a home-improvement magazine (supporting context only) explains, it shifts to compensate for its own thickness, so an outside measurement (hook pulled against an edge) and an inside measurement (hook pushed against a surface) both start from a true zero. A patent for a hinged "true zero" hook states the same goal: a true zero measurement in either mode of use. Excessive hook movement is not normal and points to a bent or worn hook.
- **The combination square.** Starrett describes the head as having three planes: a squaring face, a base holding the blade slot, and a 45-degree arm. The blade is a steel rule, and other parts include a scriber, a level vial on 6-inch and larger heads, and optional center and protractor heads. Starrett's accuracy check is the "flip" test: hold the head against a straight board edge, scribe a line along the blade, flip the head over, scribe a second line, and if the two lines are not parallel the head needs adjustment. [Walkthrough 2](/tools/walkthroughs/2-check-and-use-a-combination-square/) practices this.

| Tool | Carries | How it goes wrong | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tape measure | A length | A bent or worn hook; misreading fractions | Check hook movement; read whole inches plus a fraction, and say the number back |
| Combination square | A right angle | Dropped or damaged so the head no longer squares | Do the flip test before you rely on it; store it cleaned and lightly protected |

### Saws: rows of tiny chisels and knives

A handsaw is a row of small cutters. TM 9-867, the U.S. War Department's 1945 hand tool care manual, gives the best short description: rip saw teeth are shaped and filed to cut like chisels (for cutting with the grain), and crosscut saw teeth are shaped and filed to cut like knives (for cutting across the grain). A saw also has **set**: the teeth are bent outward alternately to the right and left so the cut (the kerf) is wider than the blade and the blade does not bind or stick.

How saws fail, per TM 9-867:

- Teeth of different heights: low teeth do not cut, and high teeth dig in and make the saw jump.
- Sawing into nails dulls, bends, or breaks teeth.
- Twisting off waste strips with the blade dulls and bends the teeth and may spring the blade.
- Letting the waste side break off as the cut finishes splits the work; support the waste and cut all the way through.
- Laying saws on the floor or in a loose toolbox dulls or bends them; hang them up.

CCOHS adds the technique: check the stock for nails and knots, hold the work firmly, start the cut with your hand beside the mark and your thumb upright against the blade, make the first strokes slow and short so the blade does not jump, apply pressure on the downstroke only, and use the whole length of the blade. Protect the teeth when the saw is not in use. For a hacksaw, install the blade with the teeth pointing forward.

TM 9-867 also gives a rule for metal-cutting blades: pick the tooth pitch so that at least two teeth are on the metal at any moment. Teeth too fine clog with chips, and teeth too coarse straddle the work.

### Chisels and planes: a wedge you steer

A chisel is a wedge with a sharp end. The slanted face that forms the edge is the **bevel**, and its length is a trade: a long, thin bevel slices easily but the fine tip nicks and dulls, while a short, blunt one is strong but has to shove wood aside. TM 9-867 says the bevel length should be about twice the thickness of the chisel near its edge; a longer bevel suits soft wood but the edge nicks and dulls easily, and a bevel that is too blunt will not cut easily and is hard to sharpen.

CCOHS's chisel rules:

- Cut with the blade pointing away from you, with your hands and body behind the cutting edge, and never hold the work in one hand while chiseling with the other.
- Secure the work so it cannot shift.
- Use a wooden or plastic mallet on standard chisels; steel hammers only on heavy-duty framing chisels. Make finishing cuts with hand pressure alone.
- Do not use a chisel as a pry bar or wedge, on metal, or with a mushroomed or chipped edge.
- Store chisels bevel side up on the bench, and in caps, rolls, or slotted pouches.

**Where sources disagree.** CCOHS advises against grinders on heat-treated tools and recommends whetstones. TM 9-867 describes grinding, but only with the tool dipped in water often, because burning softens the steel. The lesson is the same in both: heat ruins an edge, so this guide has you sharpen by hand on stones. [Walkthrough 5](/tools/walkthroughs/5-sharpen-a-chisel/) covers this.

### Hammers: borrowing your arm's momentum

A hammer lends a nail your arm's swing. The energy travels through the head into the smallest area available, so the face, the handle, and your aim all have to be sound.

| Rule (CCOHS unless noted) | Why |
| --- | --- |
| When striking another tool (a chisel or punch), pick a hammer face about 12 mm (0.5 in) larger across than the tool it strikes | A face that is too small mis-hits the target. |
| Strike with the face parallel to the surface, not glancing, over, or under | A glancing blow can slip off the target. |
| Look behind and above before you swing | People and objects can be in your swing path. |
| Never strike a hammer with a hammer, or strike hard metal, stone, or concrete | Faces can shed sharp fragments. TM 9-867 adds: use a soft hammer or a block of copper, brass, fiber, or wood on hardened steel. |
| Discard hammers with a mushroomed or chipped face, or with cracks in the claw or eye | Weakened steel shatters. |
| Replace loose, cracked, or splintered handles | OSHA: the head may fly off. |
| Do not redress, grind, weld, or reheat-treat hammer heads | CCOHS lists these as prohibited. |

### Screwdrivers and drive types: key and lock

A screw's recess is a lock and the driver tip is its key. Sources agree on the first rule: match both **size and type**. CCOHS notes that Phillips and Pozidriv drivers are not interchangeable, and that the blade width of a slotted driver should match the width of the screw head. Wiha, a tool maker, lists the classic profiles (slotted, Phillips, Pozidriv) and the newer Torx and Torx Plus.

**Cam-out** is the driver popping out of the recess under torque. Wiha explains that the geometry of conical drives (Phillips and Pozidriv) creates a force that tries to push the driver out of the screw, and rates Phillips, Pozidriv, and slotted drives as more prone to cam-out and Torx-type drives as less prone. A driver that pops out gouges the screw head and the surface around it, which is how a screw becomes "stripped".

| Drive | Recess shape | Cam-out tendency (Wiha) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slotted | One straight slot | Higher |
| Phillips | Cross-shaped (TM 9-867 calls it "cross recess") | Higher |
| Pozidriv | Cross-shaped, a refinement of the cross recess | Higher |
| Torx | A lobed "wave" profile (Wiha) | Lower |

Screwdriver rules from CCOHS and TM 9-867: keep the shank in line with the screw; use firm but not excessive force, because properly matched screws self-position; never hammer a screw that will not turn; do not use a screwdriver to pry, chisel, scrape, or stir; never grind a tip so it "fits" several sizes; never put pliers on the handle or blade for extra leverage; and never carry loose screwdrivers in a pocket.

### Wrenches and pliers: levers with jaws

A wrench is a lever that grips a fastener. A longer handle gives more turning force, which is exactly why misuse is dangerous. CCOHS lists the hazards as wrench slippage that makes you lose your balance, work pieces that suddenly break free, tool or work piece breakage, and strain from quick, forceful turning. Its rules:

- Pull on a wrench with a slow, steady pull. Do not push (unless you hold the tool with an open palm), and do not jerk.
- Use the right size so the jaws fit fully seated, flat, and not tilted.
- On an adjustable wrench, turn so the force goes toward the fixed (permanent) jaw, and never pull a loosely adjusted wrench.
- Do not add a pipe over the handle for leverage, and do not strike a wrench with a hammer. TM 9-867: use a longer wrench instead.
- Use pipe wrenches on pipe, not on nuts and bolts, and keep their teeth sharp and free of oil and debris.
- Discard wrenches with spread jaws or damaged box-wrench points. OSHA says not to use wrenches whose jaws are sprung to the point of slipping.

Pliers grip and cut. CCOHS says: cut at right angles and never rock the cutters or bend wire back and forth; pull rather than push; keep the jaws as parallel as possible; do not use pliers on nuts and bolts (they round the corners; use a wrench); do not use them as a hammer or with extended handles; do not cut hardened wire unless the pliers are made for it; and do not use cushion grips as electrical protection.

## Choosing between options

| Job | Best choice | Why | Worse choice and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turning a nut or bolt you can reach squarely | A wrench that fits exactly (CCOHS prefers box or socket wrenches with straight handles when possible) | Grips more of the fastener and slips less | Pliers: round the corners (CCOHS, TM 9-867) |
| A slotted screw | A blade matched to the screw head (CCOHS) | Stays seated in the slot | The wrong size: TM 9-867 says it batters screw heads and bends or breaks blades |
| Removing a stubborn screw | Correct driver, straight and firmly seated | Force goes into turning | Hammering or cheating with pliers on the handle: both are on CCOHS's "never" list |
| Cutting with the grain | Rip-tooth saw | Its teeth act like small chisels (TM 9-867) | Crosscut saw: TM 9-867 describes its knife-like teeth as made for cutting across the grain |
| Holding work | A clamp or vise | Frees both hands (OSHA) | Your hand: CCOHS says never hold the work in one hand while cutting or driving with the other |

## Common mistakes

- **Using the tool you have, not the tool for the job.** OSHA's first two rules exist because this is the most common cause of tool failure.
- **Pushing a wrench toward your knuckles.** Arrange the work so you pull.
- **Ignoring a mushroomed head or a loose handle "for now".** OSHA's examples of hazards are exactly these.
- **Sawing without checking for nails.** One nail dulls a saw for good.
- **Using one hand to hold the work.** Both CCOHS and OSHA say to secure it instead.
