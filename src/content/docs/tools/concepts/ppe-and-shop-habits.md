:::danger[Safety]
Every tool in this chapter can throw chips, make dust, cut, crush, or shock you. Two habits cover most of the risk: wear eye protection every time a tool touches material, and never work while tired, distracted, or rushed. This page is general guidance. Each tool's own manual lists the protective equipment and warnings for that exact model, and it overrides anything here. This guide does not replace a safety course or a person who can watch you work.
:::

## The one-line version

**Protective equipment (PPE) is the seat belt; habits are the brakes.** A seat belt does not stop the crash, it makes the crash survivable. Habits (clamped work, sharp tools, unplugged tools when you adjust them) stop the crash from happening. You want both, and you want the habits to do most of the work.

OSHA's booklet on hand and power tools boils tool safety down to five rules. They are written for workplaces, but they work as a personal checklist too:

1. Keep tools in good condition with regular maintenance.
2. Use the right tool for the job.
3. Examine each tool for damage before use, and do not use damaged tools.
4. Operate tools according to the manufacturer's instructions.
5. Use the right personal protective equipment for the task.

Rule 4 is the reason this guide keeps pointing you to your own tool's manual. The manual is the only document that describes your model.

## Protective equipment, part by part

| Part of you | What can hurt it | What to use | Where the rule comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eyes | Flying chips, sawdust, broken bit or blade fragments, splinters | Safety glasses with side protection, or goggles. A face shield goes **over** glasses or goggles, not instead of them. | OSHA 1910.133; OSHA eTool; CCOHS |
| Ears | Loud tools (saws, impact drilling, sanders) | Earplugs or earmuffs, fitted correctly | NIOSH; Milwaukee manual |
| Lungs | Wood dust, dust from paint, masonry, or treated lumber | Dust extraction where the tool has a port, good ventilation, and an approved respirator or dust mask | OSHA wood dust page; NIOSH; Milwaukee manual |
| Hands | Cuts, pinches, splinters | Gloves suited to the hazard, but see the glove note below | CCOHS; OSHA 3080 |
| Feet | Dropped tools, slips | Sturdy, non-skid footwear | OSHA 3080; Milwaukee manual |
| Hair and clothes | Being pulled into a rotating part | Tie hair back; no loose clothing, ties, or jewelry | CCOHS; Milwaukee manual |

### Eyes

OSHA's rule requires appropriate eye or face protection when flying particles are a hazard, and requires side protection when there is a flying-object hazard (detachable clip-on or slide-on side shields count). If you wear prescription glasses, the protection must either include your prescription or fit over your glasses without disturbing the lenses. OSHA's rule points to the ANSI Z87.1 standard, and the Milwaukee manual read for this page says to wear eye protection "marked to comply with ANSI Z87.1". Look for that marking. (OSHA also requires eye and face protection to be marked so the manufacturer can be identified.)

Standards change. As read on 2026-09-26, the International Safety Equipment Association announced a new edition, ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2025, published on 8 January 2026, with clearer product markings among its changes. OSHA's rule text, as read on the same date, names the 2010, 2003, and 1989 editions. OSHA's rule accepts eyewear that complies with those older editions. Either way, check the marking, and check what the tool's manual asks for.

OSHA's eTool also says eye protection needs frequent cleaning and replacement, and that metal-frame eyewear should not be used where electrical hazards exist. Replace lenses that are scratched enough that you struggle to see the cut line.

### Ears

NIOSH's recommended exposure limit is 85 A-weighted decibels (dBA) averaged over an eight-hour shift, with a 3 dB exchange rate: every 3 dBA increase in level cuts the recommended exposure time in half. Think of it like a bank account where louder noise withdraws from the same daily balance faster.

You often cannot measure the noise, so NIOSH gives a rule of thumb: you need hearing protection when you have to raise your voice to talk to someone an arm's length away.

| Type (per NIOSH) | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Earplugs | Slide into the ear canal (foam, pre-formed, or custom) |
| Earmuffs | Cover the whole outer ear, held by a headband |
| Canal caps | Cover the ear-canal openings, held by a light band |

Two points from NIOSH matter for beginners. First, the noise reduction rating printed on the package often overstates the protection a real person gets, and fit is what counts, so learn to insert plugs so they seal. Second, NIOSH says most workers need 10 dB or less of reduction, and aims for "just enough" to bring exposure to 75-85 dBA at the ear, because over-protection makes it harder to hear what is going on around you. For noise at 100 dBA or above, NIOSH recommends double protection (muffs over plugs).

### Lungs

Wood dust is not just a nuisance. OSHA's page says excessive amounts irritate the eyes, nose, and throat and can impair lung function. It is listed as a known human carcinogen by the U.S. National Toxicology Program and as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, and western red cedar dust specifically causes occupational asthma. Processed wood can also carry mold or chemical additives such as formaldehyde.

Other dust is worse in its own way. The Milwaukee manual warns that dust from sanding, sawing, drilling, and grinding can contain chemicals known to cause cancer or birth defects, and names lead from lead-based paint, crystalline silica from bricks, cement, and masonry, and arsenic and chromium from chemically treated lumber. Its advice: work in a well-ventilated area and use approved safety equipment such as dust masks designed to filter microscopic particles.

If you choose a disposable N95 respirator, NIOSH's page gives you a way to check it is real. A genuine one carries the word NIOSH, the N95 rating, a model or part number, a lot number, a TC approval number in the form TC 84A-XXXX, and the approval holder's name, and you can check the number in the NIOSH Certified Equipment List. NIOSH also says these respirators filter particles (dust, mist, fumes) and do **not** protect against gases or vapors, that they require fit testing, and that a quick user seal check before each use is not a substitute for fit testing.

### Hands, clothing, and the glove question

Sources differ, so read this carefully. CCOHS's hand-tool page says to use properly fitting gloves for hazards but to avoid bulky gloves while using hand tools. Its power-tool page says not to wear gloves, loose clothing, or jewelry while using revolving power tools, because they can catch. OSHA's booklet lists gloves and safety footwear among general practices for electric tools, in the context of protection from electrical burns and shock.

The practical resolution: for anything that spins (drills, saws, sanders), leave gloves off and keep sleeves and hair away from the rotating part unless that tool's manual says otherwise. For handling rough lumber or sharp sheet material with the tool off, gloves make sense.

## Shop habits that prevent most accidents

These come from OSHA, CCOHS, and the Milwaukee manual, which agree closely.

| Habit | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Keep the area clean and well lit. | The Milwaukee manual says cluttered or dark areas invite accidents. OSHA also says floors should be kept clean and dry so nobody slips near a dangerous tool. |
| Keep bystanders and children away. | Distractions make you lose control. |
| Clamp the work. | OSHA says securing work with clamps or a vise frees both hands to operate the tool. Never hold small work in one hand while cutting or drilling with the other (CCOHS). |
| Stay alert. | Milwaukee's warning: do not use a power tool when tired or under the influence of drugs, alcohol, or medication, because a moment of inattention can cause serious injury. It also warns not to let familiarity make you complacent. |
| Unplug or remove the battery before you adjust anything. | Blade changes, bit changes, and depth settings are the moments a tool starts by accident. Both OSHA and Milwaukee say to disconnect first. |
| Check the switch before you plug in, and carry tools without a finger on the trigger. | Prevents accidental starting. |
| Remove keys and wrenches from a tool before switching it on. | Milwaukee's manual warns a key or wrench left on a rotating part may cause injury. |
| Keep good footing and balance; do not overreach. | Gives you control if the tool behaves unexpectedly. |
| Treat cords with respect. | Never carry a tool by its cord or yank it out of the outlet, and keep cords away from heat, oil, sharp edges, and moving parts. Keep cords clear of the cutting area and out of walkways (OSHA, CCOHS, Milwaukee). |
| Manage dust. | Use extraction where the tool has a port. OSHA's wood-dust page notes that fine dust accumulations are a fire and explosion hazard, and CCOHS says the same about sanding dust and advises emptying a sander's dust collector when it is one quarter full. |
| Keep sparks away from flammables. | Milwaukee warns power tools create sparks that can ignite dust or fumes in an explosive atmosphere, and OSHA notes iron and steel hand tools can also spark. |
| Pass tools handle-first, and carry them in a toolbox, not your pockets. | CCOHS's advice for sharp tools. |
| Take damaged tools out of service. | OSHA says to remove damaged portable electric tools from use and tag them "Do Not Use". |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating glasses as optional for a "quick" cut.** The chip that hits your eye does not care how short the cut was. Put them on before you pick up the tool, and keep them on until the tool has stopped and been put down.
- **Trusting a package rating over fit.** An earplug that does not seal, or a mask that leaks around the nose, protects less than the label promises.
- **Working tired.** Milwaukee's manual, not just this page, treats fatigue as a hazard. Stop and come back.
- **Skipping the manual.** Read it once with the tool in front of you. It is the only document that describes your model.
