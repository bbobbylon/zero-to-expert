:::danger[Safety]
This job cuts nothing, so its own hazards are small. Its purpose is to build the habits every cutting job depends on: a stable, well-lit bench, a workpiece that cannot slide, and a line you can trust. A wrong or unclear line leads to a wrong cut, and people who are frustrated by a wrong cut start forcing tools. Later walkthroughs use saws, drills, and edges, and every one of them starts from the line you make here. Keep the bench clear and well lit (Milwaukee's manuals and OSHA both tie clutter and poor light to accidents), and read [protective equipment and shop habits](/tools/concepts/ppe-and-shop-habits/) before you go on.
:::

## Before you start

- Set your scrap board flat on a bench so it cannot slide. Put it where the light falls on the board, not behind your hands.
- Clear the bench of everything you do not need.
- Find the **hook** (the little metal tab on the end of the tape) and the **inch numbers** (the biggest numbers on the blade). If your tape shows other scales too, such as millimeters or stud-spacing marks, this walkthrough uses only the inch scale.
- Read [how hand tools work and fail](/tools/concepts/how-hand-tools-work-and-fail/) for the idea behind a gauge tool.

## Steps

### A. Learn the marks

The Bellevue College woodworking handout explains that inch marks are divided by halves: an inch becomes halves, quarters, eighths, sixteenths, thirty-seconds, and sixty-fourths, and each new mark is usually a shorter line than the last.

1. Pull out about a foot of tape and lay it flat along the board's edge. Find two neighboring inch numbers, for example 3 and 4. The space between them is one inch. You should see the **longest** line at each inch number.
2. Between those two inch numbers, find the next-longest line, exactly in the middle. That is the **half inch**.
3. Find the two lines halfway between the half inch and each inch number. These are the **quarter inches**, and they are shorter than the half-inch line.
4. Keep going: the lines halfway between those are **eighths**, and the shortest lines your tape has may be sixteenths (some tapes also show thirty-seconds). You should see that the shorter the line, the smaller the fraction.
5. Each time you halve a fraction, the bottom number doubles: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64 (Bellevue).

:::tip[Analogy: a street address]
Think of the numbers as street numbers and the little lines as house numbers on that street. First you find the street (whole inches), then the house (the fraction).
:::

### B. Read a measurement out loud

6. To read any point on the tape, say the **whole inches you have passed**, then the **fraction past it**. Bellevue's example: 12 13/16 inches means 12 whole inches and 13/16 of the next inch. You can find 13/16 by counting thirteen sixteenths forward from the 12, or by finding the 13 and counting **back** three sixteenths.
7. Practice with Bellevue's second example. Find 19/32 inch. Counting thirty-seconds is easy to get wrong, so use the neighbors instead: 19/32 sits between 18/32 (which reduces to 9/16) and 20/32 (which reduces to 5/8), so it is the line between the 9/16 mark and the 5/8 mark.
8. **Reduce** fractions by dividing the top and bottom by the same number: 4/16 becomes 1/4, and 2/4 becomes 1/2. Say the reduced fraction out loud so you match how tapes are read.
9. Point at five random marks on the tape and read each one. Check the reading by counting from the nearest inch number a different way.

### C. Check the hook

The metal hook on a tape is loose on purpose. A home-improvement magazine (supporting context) explains that it slides to account for its own thickness, so an **outside** measurement (hook pulled over an edge) and an **inside** measurement (hook pushed into a corner) both start from a true zero. A patent for a hinged "true zero" hook describes the same aim.

10. Pull the tape out a few inches and let it go slightly, so the hook rests against the blade. Then pull on the tape so the hook moves out. You should see the hook slide a small distance, about the thickness of the hook's own metal.
11. If the hook moves a lot more than that, is bent, or has torn loose, the magazine says this is not normal, and you should not trust the tape for careful work. Replace it.

### D. Measure and mark one line

You will mark a line 12 13/16 inches from one end of your scrap board, then find the middle of the board's width.

12. Lay the board flat with one end toward you.
13. Hook the tape over the end of the board and pull the tape **straight and taut** along the edge. Keep the tape lying flat against the board so it does not sag.
14. Read 12 13/16 inches. Make a small tick with a sharp pencil on the board right at that mark. Make the tick a thin **line across the edge**, not a dot.
15. Slide the tape to the other edge of the board (or pull a second measurement near the far edge) and make a second tick at the same 12 13/16 inches. You now have two points, and two points define exactly one line.
16. Lay your straightedge across the two ticks so its edge touches both, and draw the line with the sharp pencil. You should see one thin line crossing both ticks.
17. Mark the **waste side** (the side that will be cut away and thrown out) with a small X. This is this guide's habit, not from a listed source. It stops you from cutting on the wrong side of the line later.
18. Now find the center of the board's width using Bellevue's method. If the board is 11 3/8 inches wide, halve the whole inches (11 becomes 5 1/2) and halve the fraction (3/8 becomes 3/16, by keeping the numerator and doubling the denominator), then add: 5 1/2 plus 3/16 equals 5 8/16 plus 3/16, which is 5 11/16 inches. Measure that far from one edge and tick it.

## Check it worked

- Read the same mark twice, counting from a different inch number each time. Both readings should give the same fraction.
- Measure from the **other end** of the board to the line. The two distances (12 13/16 inches from one end, and the remainder from the other) should add up to the board's full length.
- Look along the line at eye level. It should be one thin, continuous line with no doubled or wandering pencil marks.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two readings of the same distance disagree | You counted marks from different starting points, or the hook shifted | Recount from the nearest inch number. Pull the tape taut so the hook is fully extended for outside measurements. |
| You cannot tell 1/8 from 1/16 | The line-length pattern is unfamiliar | Find the half-inch and quarter-inch lines first; they are the longest after the inch. Work outward from them (Bellevue: 1/2 and 1/4 marks are easy reference points). |
| You keep miscounting 32nds | Counting is hard when marks get small | Use Bellevue's neighbor method: find the two larger fractions on each side and place the mark between them. |
| The tape sags or curls and the reading seems off | The blade is not lying flat or is unsupported | Lay the tape along the board's edge and keep it taut. |
| The hook moves a lot, or the tape reads differently when you push it against a surface | The hook is loose, bent, or worn | Replace the tape for careful work; the magazine source says excessive hook movement is not normal. |
| The fraction will not reduce | You need a common factor | Divide the top and bottom by the same number, such as 2 or 4, until no number divides both (Bellevue). |
| The pencil line looks fat or fuzzy | Dull pencil | Re-sharpen; see the TODO(source) note about marking tools. |
